# Meds Addis — Design Context

Source of truth for every visual decision in this project. If a change cannot be
justified against this file, it does not belong in the UI.

## Users

**Two distinct audiences, one system.**

1. **Patients in Addis Ababa**, mostly on a mid-range Android phone over a
   mobile connection, often while unwell or caring for someone who is. They are
   not browsing — they are looking for a specific medicine, checking whether it
   is in stock, and trying to get it today. They need to trust that what arrives
   is genuine and correctly dispensed. Their patience for decoration is zero.

2. **Pharmacists and dispensary staff** on a desktop, working a queue of
   prescription reviews and fulfilment tasks. They need density, scannability
   and unambiguous state. They will use this screen a hundred times a day.

The job to be done is *certainty*: is this the right medicine, is it real, will
it arrive, is this prescription valid.

## Brand Personality

**Regulated. Precise. Unhurried.**

The voice is a pharmacist who has done this for twenty years: plain, exact,
never salesy, never alarming. It states facts and lets you decide. It does not
say "Oops!" and it does not say "Awesome!". It says what happened and what to do
next.

Emotional goal: the interface should feel like it is on your side and cannot be
rushed into an error. Calm authority, not friendliness.

## Aesthetic Direction

**Clinical Swiss.** A regulated medical label, not a consumer app. The Swiss
International Style applied to a pharmacopoeia: information density over
decoration, rules over boxes, one signal colour over a palette.

The reference objects are physical, not digital: a medicine's patient
information leaflet, a dispensing label, a laboratory specification sheet, a
printed form with numbered sections and hairline rules.

### Concrete commitments

| Dimension | Decision |
|---|---|
| **Type** | Archivo for everything structural. IBM Plex Mono for anything that is data — batch numbers, expiry, order IDs, prices, dosages, section numbers. |
| **Colour** | Near-monochrome. A cool neutral ramp with a trace of blue. **Prescription amber is the only signal colour** and is used sparingly enough that it still means something. |
| **Primary action** | Near-black ink, not the brand colour. Amber is reserved for prescription and attention states, which is the only way it stays meaningful. |
| **Depth** | Hairline rules and whitespace. No drop shadows. No gradients. |
| **Radius** | Zero, everywhere. Sharp rectangles read as a printed form. Circles only for status dots. |
| **Rhythm** | Sections separated by full-width rules and numbered in mono (01, 02, 03), like a specification sheet. Not an uppercase eyebrow above every heading. |
| **Layout** | An asymmetric 12-column grid with deliberate offsets. Not centred, not symmetric. |
| **Motion** | State changes only. Exponential ease-out. Nothing bounces, nothing floats. |
| **Icons** | Hand-written inline SVG at a single stroke weight. Never emoji. |

### Anti-references

Things this must explicitly **not** look like:

- **The Next.js starter.** Geist, a teal accent, a dark gradient hero, and
  rounded cards with soft shadows. This is what the project looked like before
  this document existed.
- **A generic SaaS dashboard.** Frosted glass, pill badges, gradient overlays,
  a coloured card for every metric.
- **A wellness app.** Soft pastels, rounded everything, photography of smiling
  people, and the word "journey".
- **Brutalism for its own sake.** Sharp corners and heavy rules are a
  consequence of the printed-form metaphor, not a style in search of a
  justification. Legibility wins every time.

### What makes an interface read as AI-generated

The problem is never ugliness — it is *undifferentiation*. AI output is the
statistical average of everything in its training data, so it lands on the same
safe choices every time. The specific tells:

1. The `create-next-app` default font stack.
2. A saturated accent (teal, indigo) on a dark gradient hero.
3. Emoji standing in for icons.
4. Every container being the same rounded, softly shadowed card.
5. A hero stat block — four big numbers in a 2×2 grid.
6. An uppercase eyebrow above every single section heading.
7. The identical rhythm repeated down the page: eyebrow, heading, paragraph,
   grid. Five sections, five times.
8. Copy with no point of view — true, grammatical, and interchangeable.

The fix is not to add more. It is to make specific choices and let them
constrain everything else.

## Design Principles

1. **Every colour must earn its place.** A colour that appears in ten places
   communicates nothing in any of them. Amber appears only where the user's
   attention is genuinely required.
2. **Rules, not boxes.** A hairline separates without adding weight. A card
   around everything is a decision not to decide.
3. **The type is the design.** Hierarchy comes from scale, weight and space. Not
   from colour, decoration, or iconography.
4. **Data is set in mono.** Anything a user might compare, copy or verify —
   prices, dates, batch numbers, IDs — aligns in tabular figures so it can be
   read as a column.
5. **Density is respect.** A pharmacist reviewing a queue should see twenty
   orders, not four large cards. Whitespace is for the marketing surface, not
   for the tool.
6. **State is stated, never implied.** Never rely on colour alone to carry
   meaning. Every status has a word next to it.
7. **Accessibility is structural.** 44px targets, 4.5:1 body contrast, visible
   focus, real buttons and forms. Not a checklist applied at the end.
